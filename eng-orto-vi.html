She is the initiator of the ORTOTATTILE series (2023-ongoing), a participatory food-art performance project investigating sensory perception, collective rituals, and the politics of visibility.

ORTOTATTILE VI: Bao Bread Bao


《ORTOTATTILE》 (Italian for “Tactile Garden”) is a series that has sought to dismantle the hierarchy of the senses governed by ocularcentrism and to construct a density of non-visual perception where touch and hearing intersect. The series intervenes in what Jacques Rancière terms the distribution of the sensible—the allocation of what can be perceived, by whom, and under what conditions¹—bringing to the foreground those senses historically subordinated to vision. If Western epistemological traditions have long positioned touch beneath sight,² 《ORTOTATTILE》 is not simply an inversion of that hierarchy but a recurring practice that attempts to render the hierarchy itself inoperative.

                        Where the first five iterations of the participatory food-art performance workshop series 《ORTOTATTILE》 ³ were conducted as closed workshops with pre-registered participants, 〈ORTOTATTILE VI: Bao Bread Bao〉 unfolds as a guerrilla-style performance workshop on a staircase near PMQ in Central, Hong Kong, inviting anyone passing through the street to become a participant. The site is not merely a backdrop. A staircase is not a destination but a space designed for passage. This infrastructure of movement—through which countless bodies circulate without remembering one another—partially reveals the conditions of what Marc Augé calls the non-place.⁴ Layered with humidity and heat, the stairways of Central function both as spaces of vertical movement and as circuits of perpetual circulation connecting sea level to the stratified topography of the city. Into this non-place, the work inserts a temporary pause. As an everyday tactic that introduces a fissure into the logic of institutionalized space,⁵ the moment passersby stop and touch bread together with strangers, a temporary tactile garden emerges within an anonymous space of transit.

The central materials of this work are bao (包) and butter. The Chinese character 包 carries meanings such as “to wrap,” “to hold,” and “to embrace,” and its etymology derives from the image of a person holding a child. Hong Kong’s iconic local pastry, bolo bao (菠蘿包), materially embodies this history of wrapping. It is a hybrid product formed through the intersection of Western baking traditions introduced during British colonial rule, Cantonese food culture, and multiple cultural trajectories extending through regions such as Mexico and Japan.⁶ Irreducible to any singular cultural lineage, this bread condenses the historical conditions that have shaped Hong Kong as a city. Cultural identity is not an essential origin but an ongoing process of negotiation between past and present;⁷ bolo bao may thus be understood as a material trace of that negotiation.⁸

The installation occupies the site prior to the performance as a self-contained structure. On metal stands rests a cluster of loaves in which Western sourdough bread is wrapped and secured with bolo bao. The wires binding the sourdough to the bolo bao vibrate like the strings of an electric guitar when touched, producing sound. Above the breads sits a block of ice resting on a cotton cloth. Using droppers, the artist slowly releases Korean styrax honey, chestnut blossom honey, chestnut allulose syrup, Italian olive oil, and Greek balsamic vinegar onto the ice. As the ice melts, the liquids flow downward together, permeating both the surface and interior of the bread. This movement remains beyond the artist’s control. The speed of melting, the direction of flow, and the degree to which particular loaves absorb the liquids are all determined by temperature, humidity, and gravity. Flowing from top to bottom within the vertical structure of the staircase, this system incorporates the topographical stratification of Central into the physical logic of the performance. As materials of different origins melt, mingle, and seep into the bread, hybridity appears not as a declaration but as a material process.

Participants use no tools. Wearing only sanitary gloves, they spread butter across the bao and decorate it collectively with dried cherry tomatoes, dried kumquats, black volcanic salt, red pepper, and dried edible flowers. This gesture begins with bolo yau (菠蘿油), one of Hong Kong’s most iconic snacks.⁹ In bolo yau, a slab of cold butter is inserted into a hot bolo bao and slowly melts into its interior. In this work as well, yau (油)—oil or fat—travels across the surface and interior of the bread through the process of thawing. Butter and olive oil, honey and vinegar, become agents that connect and infiltrate materials of disparate origins. Here, however, butter is no longer inserted into the bread. Instead, it is spread across and shared upon its surface. The act of wrapping (包) is transformed into a ritual gesture of preparing a communal table. The hand does not merely touch; it traces the surface of history. The absence of tools demands direct contact between skin and material, and within that immediacy, the hand ceases to function as a mere instrument and becomes a sensing subject.


Embedded within the bread are piezo disc microphones connected through a Zoom recorder and effects processors to a single pair of headphones. While multiple hands touch the bread and its wires, only one person can listen to those tactile actions as sound. Vibrations produced by pressing and stroking the bread are transformed through electronic processing, while fingers plucking the wires generate both raw string resonance and modified sound simultaneously. The plurality of sounds produced by other people’s hands approaches the headphone listener as the presence of invisible others. The sensory asymmetry produced by a single set of headphones involves feeling without seeing and receiving the touch of others through one’s body without touching oneself. Separate, yet together.¹⁰ This paradox takes material form as a sensory condition within the work. The temporary community that emerges within this non-place dissolves without trace once the bread is consumed and the ice has completely melted away. What remains is only the memory of sensation absorbed into each participant’s body.


〈ORTOTATTILE VI: Bao Bread Bao〉 is the sixth garden in the 《ORTOTATTILE》 series, generating a temporary sense of place within a space organized for movement. On the stairways of Hong Kong, ice melts, liquids flow and permeate, and the hands of strangers meet upon a single loaf of bread, leaving traces behind. Some touch, some listen, and some move between the two. In doing so, the work transforms sensation from a form of individual possession into an experience produced collectively.

What remains after the bread has been consumed and the ice has disappeared is not a finished form. It is the trace left by a fleeting convergence of space, time, and bodies passing through one another. Emerging only briefly within a non-place before vanishing again, this sensory community reveals that sensation is never entirely one’s own but is formed through contact with others. 包. To wrap. To hold. To embrace. What is wrapped in this work is not bread but relation; what is held is not material but the sensations of others.


(Text by Heeju Kim)

The precise origins of bolo bao (菠蘿包; Cantonese: bō lòh bāau) remain debated. It is generally understood to have emerged through the encounter between Western baking techniques introduced during Hong Kong’s colonial period and food traditions of southern China. Scholars have also noted formal similarities between bolo bao, the Mexican concha, and the Japanese melon pan, suggesting broader transnational circulations of baking techniques and food cultures across the Pacific during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. However, attributing its origin to any single cultural source remains difficult.

               EN
Heeju Kim, based in Seoul, is an independent curator and interdisciplinary artist who explores the boundaries of visual art through the intersection of the visual, auditory, and tactile. Her works, Ground of Vibration (2024) and Ground of Vibration: Aqueous Layer (2024), envision alternative resonances through vibration, moving beyond the binary of the human and non-human. In her participatory food-art performance workshop ORTOTATTILE (2023), she reinterpreted early 20th-century Italian Futurist formulas/recipes with her own formulas to propose multi-sensory perception. The project expanded into ORTOTATTILE II: Baguette of Solidarities (2024), an experimental inquiry into solidarity and collective voice in democratic society, using the density of ingredients embedded within baguette cross-sections as a medium. ORTOTATTILE IV: (non-)transparent catering (2025) examines the duality embedded within museum systems, institutional labor, and formats through the framework of catering.


From 2020 to 2021, she was active as HjkEg Collective and presented artworks focusing on human emotions and interaction with nature at the Ars Electronica 2021, the Bucheon International Fantastic Film Festival(BIFAN), etc. In 2022, as one of the curators of the team PACK, she took part in the curatorial planning and production of several projects including "Hinterland" (2021-2022), "Hinterland: The Second Story" (2022), and "PACK WEEK 2022" (2022). And she contributed to the planning and production of the works, PACK 〈Hinterland: Future Fossil〉(2022) and PACK 〈Hinterland: The Anatomy of a Houseboat〉(2022) in the Busan Biennale 2022, We, on the Rising Wave.


In 2024, she was selected as a participant in the "M+ x ACC workshop" program by M+ Museum in Hong Kong and the National Asia Culture Center (ACC), receiving support to participate in overseas workshops. She was also chosen as one of the final emerging curators for the "Gwangju Biennale Academy 2024 International Curator Course (GBICC)" and participated in the associated workshop with the 15th Gwangju Biennale, PANSORI: A Soundscape of the 21st Century.
